### Abstract

My predecessor professor Donald G. Frantz left his entire archive of Indigenous language recordings and other invaluable materials in his office on campus before he passed away, and his family and the university left it to me to deal with. Between 2022 and 2026 I spent a significant amount of my time working with local Blackfoot research assistants, fluent speakers and Elders to inventory, catalogue, digitize and describe the materials in preparation for archiving and community distribution.

In this talk I will discuss some of the practical and ethical dilemmas we encountered while working with the materials and how we addressed them. A colleague recently called it “a crisis in documentary linguistics,” referring to similar legacy collections at risk of being lost if not handled appropriately (Johnson 2004). A central challenge in deciding what to do is that what was considered appropriate in the mid-20th century is not what is considered good (i.e. ethical) practice in the 21st century (Smythe Kung et al., n.d.; Brandão et al. 2023).

Working through my predecessor’s archive made me think about what I am potentially inflicting on those who come after me – or, perhaps more likely, what might happen to my own data collections if there is no one to undertake that work. I will suggest that we all should have some sort of data will in order to ensure, first, that we do not inflict this type of work on our own successors, and, second, that our life’s work does not go to waste.
